Testing the claims

Engine Hire says its app boosts retention by 60% and cuts communication time by 75%. What would it take to believe that?

What the evidence looks like today

Almost everything comes from Engine Hire's own pages. The App Store listing has no ratings or reviews yet, and GetApp holds five reviews with little detail. So the honest position is: plausible, not demonstrated.

Why before-and-after isn't enough

Comparing a metric before and after an intervention has no control, so it can't separate the product's effect from other changes or natural trends (Statsig). A confounder is a third factor that moves the outcome too (Spotify Confidence). A good causal claim names the counterfactual: what would have happened without the product.

Two fixes. Random assignment spreads other factors evenly across groups. When you can't randomise, roll the change out to some branches and not others, then compare the change in each group, which cancels anything affecting both equally (same source).

Practice

An agency adopts the app in January and no-shows fall in February. What is missing?

Without a comparison group you can't rule out seasonal or other changes.

An agency also raised pay rates in January. This pay rise is a:

Higher pay could itself reduce no-shows, muddying the app's effect.

Which design gives the strongest evidence that the app reduces no-shows?

Treated and untreated branches share the same season and market, so comparing their changes isolates the app's effect (ideally with random choice of branches).

Apply it

Pick one claim: "60% better retention". In the box, write (1) the metric, (2) the baseline you'd collect, (3) the comparison group, (4) one confounder to watch for.

One good answerMetric: share of workers still taking shifts after 90 days. Baseline: the same figure for the prior 6 months. Comparison: branches not yet on the app. Confounder: a pay-rate change or a seasonal peak. Also ask how the vendor defined "retention".
